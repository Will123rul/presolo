01

Paperwork

Two questions that look alike and are not. One is about the airplane, one is about you.

02

What documents do you need on the airplane?

Short answer

ARROW — the documents that must be aboard the aircraft.

AAirworthiness certificate — must be displayed where it's legible to passengers and crew. No expiration date; valid as long as the aircraft is maintained per the regs.
RRegistration — current. (Registrations expire and get renewed; check the date.)
RRadio station license — only required for international flight. Not needed domestically.
OOperating limitations — the POH/AFM, plus placards, instrument markings, and any supplements. All three together make up the limitations.
WWeight & balance data — current, specific to this tail number, including the equipment list.

Where it comes from

14 CFR §91.203 (certificate and registration), §91.9 (operating limitations aboard), §91.203(a)(2). The radio station license is an FCC requirement, not FAA.

The follow-up they'll ask

"How do you know it's airworthy?" — that's AV1ATE: Airworthiness directives complied with, VOR check (IFR only, 30 days), 100-hour inspection (if for hire or flight instruction for hire), Annual inspection (12 calendar months), Transponder (24 calendar months), ELT (12 calendar months, or 1 hour cumulative use, or ½ battery life). Know where each of those is recorded in the maintenance logs.

03

What documents do you need with you?

5 pts · Short answer

Three things on your person, and for a student solo, a fourth that matters just as much.

1Student pilot certificate — the plastic card from the FAA. It does not expire.
2Medical certificate — at least third class, current. For a student solo this is what makes the student pilot certificate usable.
3Government-issued photo ID — driver's license or passport. Explicitly required by §61.3(a)(2).
4Logbook with your endorsements — your §61.87 pre-solo endorsement and the 90-day solo endorsement live here. On a solo cross-country you must carry it; carry it every solo anyway.

Where it comes from

§61.3(a) and (c) — certificate, medical, photo ID. §61.51(i) — student pilots must present the logbook with the required endorsements on solo cross-country flights. §61.87(n) and §61.93(c) — the endorsements themselves, and the 90-day currency on the solo endorsement.

Easy point to lose

People forget the photo ID. It is a separate, explicit regulatory requirement — not just a practical suggestion.

02

Airspace

One question, but the reasoning behind it is worth more than the letter.

04

What airspace is Aurora?

5 pts · Multiple choice

A  ·  B  ·  C  ·  D  ·  E

Class D. Aurora Municipal (KARR) has an operating control tower and no radar approach control of its own — that's the definition of Class D.

How to reason to it, not memorize it

  • Class B — the big ones. O'Hare (KORD) and Midway (KMDW) are the Class B/C airspace near you. Solid blue lines on the sectional. Students are prohibited from KORD and other §91.131(b)(1) airports entirely.
  • Class C — tower + radar + a certain traffic volume. Magenta solid lines.
  • Class D — tower, no radar approach control. Dashed blue on the sectional. That's Aurora.
  • Class E — controlled but no tower at the surface; dashed magenta if it goes to the surface, faded magenta shading where it starts at 700 AGL.

The question behind the question

Class D exists only while the tower is open. When KARR tower closes, the airspace reverts — usually to Class E to the surface (if weather reporting and the required infrastructure remain) or Class G. Look up KARR's tower hours in the Chart Supplement, and know the ceiling printed in the dashed blue box on the sectional. Your CFI may well ask, "It's 2200 local, what airspace are you in now, and who do you talk to?"

Entry requirements — Class D

Two-way radio communication established with the tower before entry (§91.129). "Established" means they used your callsign without a restriction — "Skyhawk 12345, standby" counts; "aircraft calling, standby" does not. Weather minimums are 3 SM visibility and 1,000 above / 500 below / 2,000 horizontal from clouds. No transponder required for Class D itself, but you're under the KORD Mode C veil — check whether KARR falls inside the 30 NM ring on your chart.

What source(s) do you use to get a weather brief?

5 pts · Short answer

Name an official source first, then your supplements. The word "source(s)" is a hint that they want more than one.

Official / loggable

  • 1800wxbrief.com or 1-800-WX-BRIEF — Leidos Flight Service. This is the standard-briefing source and it creates a record that you got a briefing.
  • aviationweather.gov — NWS Aviation Weather Center. METARs, TAFs, graphical forecasts, PIREPs, AIRMET/SIGMET.

Supplemental

  • ForeFlight / Garmin Pilot — most EFBs can file a Leidos briefing through the app, which makes it official rather than just supplemental.
  • ATIS at KARR, and AWOS/ASOS at your destination or alternates.
  • Looking outside and talking to a pilot who just landed — a live PIREP is worth a lot.

Say the three briefing types

Standard (the full picture — use it), Abbreviated (updating or filling a gap in a brief you already have), Outlook (departure is 6+ hours out). §91.103 requires you to become familiar with all available information concerning the flight — a weather brief is how you do that.

07

Is this good flying weather?

2 pts · Yes / No

The answer depends on the METAR they showed you — but the reasoning is what earns the points, and it's the same every time. Run it against three gates, in this order.

Gate 1 — the regs for a student solo

  • Flight and surface visibility at least 3 SM by day, 5 SM at night (§61.89(a)(6)–(7)).
  • You must be able to fly by visual reference to the surface (§61.89(a)(8)).
  • Class D VFR minimums: 3 SM, and 1,000 ft above / 500 ft below / 2,000 ft horizontal from clouds.
  • No solo in Class B airspace or at the §91.131(b)(1) airports without specific training and endorsement.

Gate 2 — your endorsement

Your CFI's solo endorsement almost certainly carries limits that are tighter than the regs — a maximum surface wind, a maximum crosswind component, a ceiling and visibility floor, and often "no gusts above X." Those are the real limits. Write yours in here so it's in front of you:

Gate 3 — crosswind, actually computed

Wind is pulled from the METAR you decoded above. Edit the runways if these don't match the current Chart Supplement.

Computed against the gust when one is reported — that's the number that matters on short final. Crosswind = speed × sin(angle), headwind = speed × cos(angle).

How to write the answer

Don't just circle Yes or No. Write the reason: "No — 2 SM in mist is below the 3 SM student solo minimum, and the 700 ft overcast is below my endorsement's 3,000 ft ceiling." Or: "Yes — 10 SM, ceiling 12,000 broken, and the 270/12G18 gives 8 kt of crosswind on Runway 27, inside my 10 kt limit." That's the answer that earns full credit and, more to the point, it's the habit that keeps you alive.

Aerodynamics

Two open questions where the trap is answering too briefly.

08

Explain lift

5 pts · Short answer

Give both explanations. Answering with only Bernoulli is the single most common way to leave points on the table here.

Newton — the one that actually carries the weight

The wing meets the air at a positive angle of attack and deflects a large mass of air downward. Every action has an equal and opposite reaction, so the air pushes the wing up. Downwash is the visible evidence.

Bernoulli — the pressure picture

Air moving over the curved upper surface travels faster than the air beneath. In a streamline, an increase in velocity comes with a decrease in static pressure — so pressure on top is lower than pressure below, and the net pressure difference is an upward force.

Then close with the equation

L = CL × ½ρV² × S

  • CL — coefficient of lift, which the pilot changes with angle of attack and flaps
  • ρ — air density (falls with altitude, heat, and humidity)
  • V — velocity, and note it's squared — double the speed, four times the lift
  • S — wing surface area

The two things you control in flight are angle of attack and airspeed.

Where this is going

The follow-up is always the stall. A wing stalls when it exceeds its critical angle of attack — and it will do that at any airspeed, any attitude, any power setting, and any weight. That last sentence is the one to have memorized.

09

What are the left turning tendencies?

5 pts · Short answer

Four of them. Name each, say what causes it, and say when you feel it.

PP-factor (asymmetric propeller loading). At a high angle of attack the descending blade on the right side takes a bigger bite of air than the ascending blade on the left, so the right side of the disc makes more thrust and the nose yaws left. Felt: climbs, slow flight, takeoff.
AAccelerating — the condition that brings the rest on. Some texts use the A for the accelerated slipstream; the useful point is that all of these bite hardest at high power, low airspeed, high angle of attack.
SSpiraling slipstream. The prop wash corkscrews around the fuselage and strikes the left side of the vertical stabilizer, pushing the tail right and yawing the nose left. Felt: strongest at high power and low speed — the takeoff roll.
TTorque. The prop turns clockwise from the cockpit, so by Newton's third law the airframe wants to roll left. On the ground this puts weight on the left main, and the extra friction yaws you left too. Felt: any power application.

And the fourth, which the mnemonic hides

Gyroscopic precession. The spinning propeller is a gyroscope: a force applied to it is felt 90° ahead in the direction of rotation. Push the nose over and the force applied at the top of the disc shows up on the right side — yawing the nose left. Felt: pitch changes; dramatic in a tailwheel airplane when the tail comes up, mild in a 172.

The answer they want next

Right rudder. And the reason the airplane is rigged the way it is: the engine is mounted with a slight right offset and the vertical stabilizer is offset, so the tendencies cancel out at cruise. That's why you need almost no rudder in level cruise and a bootful on a full-power climb.

The sentence to write

"A [Lycoming IO-360-L2A]: a four-cylinder, horizontally opposed, air-cooled, fuel-injected, normally aspirated, direct-drive engine producing [180] horsepower at [2700] RPM."

Decode the model number — it's free points

In IO-360: I = fuel injected (an O-320 with no I is carbureted), O = horizontally opposed, 360 = displacement in cubic inches. Know whether yours has an I. If it does, you have no carburetor heat — and that changes your answer to the rough-engine question two cards down.

Be ready for the four-stroke cycle

Intake, compression, power, exhaust — "suck, squeeze, bang, blow." Two crankshaft revolutions per complete cycle. And the ignition system: two magnets, engine-driven and completely independent of the electrical system, each firing one of two spark plugs per cylinder. That gives you redundancy and a more complete burn — which is exactly why the RPM drops slightly when you check each mag individually.

11

Describe the fuel system

5 pts · Short answer

Trace the fuel from the filler cap to the cylinder. If you can narrate the path, you can answer any version of this question.

The path — high-wing Cessna

  1. Two vented wing tanks, one per wing. The vents matter: block a vent and fuel stops flowing as a vacuum forms.
  2. Gravity feed. The tanks are above the engine, so no fuel pump is required for the engine to run. This is the headline fact for a high-wing.
  3. Fuel selector valve — BOTH, LEFT, RIGHT, OFF. Normal operation in a 172 is BOTH.
  4. Fuel shutoff valve — cuts fuel to the engine; part of the engine fire checklist.
  5. Fuel strainer / gascolator — the low point of the system, where water and sediment collect. This is the drain you sump on preflight along with the tank drains.
  6. Injected: engine-driven fuel pump → fuel/air control unit → flow divider → injector nozzle at each cylinder. An electric auxiliary pump is used for starting and as a backup.
    Carbureted: → carburetor, where fuel is drawn into the venturi and mixed with air, plus a primer for starting.
  7. Mixture control sets fuel-to-air ratio; you lean as you climb because air density falls while fuel flow doesn't.

The numbers to know cold

  • Capacity — total and usable. They're different, and the difference is unusable fuel that shows on the gauge but won't reach the engine. 172S example: 56 total, 53 usable.
  • Grade and color — 100LL, dyed blue. 100 octane, low lead. Jet A is clear and smells like kerosene; if a truck ever puts it in your wing, you'll see the dye wash out.
  • Fuel burn in cruise, from Section 5 of the POH — you need this for endurance and for the weight and balance question.

Preflight follow-up they'll ask

"Why do you sump?" — to check for water (sinks to the bottom, appears as clear beads or a distinct line), sediment, and correct grade (blue). And "why sump all the drains?" — because water pools at whichever low point the airplane's attitude puts it in; one drain isn't proof.

06

Emergencies

A two-part scenario. The second half is the one people fumble, because it isn't about flying.

12

Your engine is running rough. What do you do?

5 pts · Short answer

Lead with the first three words, always: fly the airplane. Then troubleshoot the things that feed the engine, in order.

Step 0 — before you touch anything

Aviate. Navigate. Communicate. Pitch for best glide, pick a field, and start turning toward it before you begin troubleshooting. A rough engine can become no engine while your head is down.

Carbureted (O-320)Fuel-injected (IO-360)
Carburetor heat — ON. Carb ice is the most likely cause and the most likely thing you can fix. Expect the engine to run rougher for a few seconds as the melted ice passes through, then smooth out. That roughness is the confirmation, not a reason to turn it off. No carb heat — injected engines don't ice the same way. Reach for alternate air if induction blockage is suspected (many are automatic).
Mixture — adjust. Full rich first; if that doesn't help, lean it. Roughness from an over-rich or over-lean mixture is common and instantly reversible.
Fuel selector — BOTH, or switch tanks. Confirm the fuel shutoff is in.
—Auxiliary fuel pump — ON. Covers an engine-driven pump failure.
Magnetos — check L, then R, then BOTH. If it runs smoother on one mag, leave it there — you have a fouled plug or a bad mag. Fouled plugs sometimes clear with aggressive leaning at higher power.
Engine instruments — scan. Oil pressure and temperature, CHT, EGT, fuel flow, ammeter. This tells you whether it's fuel, ignition, or something mechanical.

Then commit to a decision

Land as soon as practical. An engine that ran rough once will run rough again, and there is nothing to be gained by pressing on. Tell ATC — at KARR, tell the tower; away from the field, use 121.5 or whoever you're already talking to. Say "Mayday" if the engine is failing, "Pan-pan" if it's degraded but running. Squawk 7700. Nobody has ever been disciplined for declaring an emergency they didn't strictly need; people have died avoiding the word.

The authority you already have

§91.3(b): in an in-flight emergency requiring immediate action, the pilot in command may deviate from any rule in Part 91 to the extent required to meet that emergency. You do not need anyone's permission first.

13

You successfully handle the situation above. You land anywhere but back at ARR. What do you do?

5 pts · Short answer

The trap in this question is the temptation to fly home. You do not fly the airplane again. Everything else follows from that.

1Secure the airplane. Mixture idle cutoff, fuel selector off, mags off, master off. Get clear of the runway or movement area if you can do it safely. Chock and tie down if you're staying.
2Make sure everyone knows you're down and safe. If you declared, ATC and search-and-rescue are watching for you — close that loop. If you were on flight following or had a flight plan, close it, or an alert notice goes out and people start looking.
3Call your CFI and the flight school. Immediately. Before you call anyone else, before you post anything. Your instructor decides what happens to the airplane and to you.
4Do not fly it home. Not "it seems fine now," not "it was just carb ice." A mechanic has to look at it and sign it off. Beyond the airworthiness question, your solo endorsement authorized a specific flight — it does not authorize you to ferry a broken airplane back from an unfamiliar field.
5Write it all down while it's fresh. Times, altitudes, indications, what you did and in what order, who you talked to. Do this within the hour. Your memory of it is worse than you think and it will keep getting worse.

The reporting rules — know that these exist

  • §91.3(c) — if you deviated from a rule under emergency authority, you must send a written report to the FAA if the Administrator asks for one. Not automatically; on request.
  • NTSB Part 830 — immediate notification is required for an accident (substantial damage, or a serious injury or death) or for certain serious incidents, which include a complete loss of engine power requiring an off-airport landing. A precautionary landing at an airport with the engine still running is generally neither.
  • NASA ASRS — file the report at asrs.arc.nasa.gov. It's confidential, and a timely filing can waive the sanction for an inadvertent violation. Filing costs you nothing.

The thing that actually matters

If you handled an engine problem and got the airplane on the ground with nobody hurt, you did the job. Students sometimes hide these events out of embarrassment. Don't. Call your instructor and tell them everything, including the parts you'd rather not.

14

What is the definition and speed for Vx?

5 pts · Short answer

Best angle of climb — the greatest gain in altitude per unit of horizontal distance.

Your number: —

When you use it

Short field takeoff, and any time there's an obstacle off the departure end. It gets you the most height in the least ground covered — you clear the trees. It's slower and it climbs less per minute, so you come off it as soon as the obstacle is behind you.

Aerodynamically

Vx is where excess thrust is greatest. As you climb, Vx increases with altitude while Vy decreases — the two converge at the airplane's absolute ceiling, where excess power is zero and you can no longer climb at all.

Keep them straight

X for the x-axis — horizontal distance. Angle. Trees.
Y for the y-axis — altitude over time. Rate. Cruise climb.

15

What is the definition and speed for Vy?

5 pts · Short answer

Best rate of climb — the greatest gain in altitude per unit of time.

Your number: —

When you use it

The normal takeoff climb, and any time you just want altitude quickly with nothing in the way. It's the everyday climb speed.

Aerodynamically

Vy is where excess power is greatest. It decreases with altitude, meeting Vx at the absolute ceiling.

Say this and you'll sound like you understand it

"Vx gets me over the obstacle in the least distance; Vy gets me to altitude in the least time. On a normal departure at Aurora I use Vy, unless there's something to clear off the end — then Vx until I'm past it, then lower the nose to Vy."

16

What is the definition and speed for Vso?

5 pts · Short answer

The stalling speed, or minimum steady flight speed, in the landing configuration — full flaps, gear down if retractable, power off, at maximum gross weight.

Your number: —

Read the subscript

The S is stall. The 0 means zero flap retraction — flaps fully extended, i.e. the landing configuration. Compare Vs1, where the 1 means a specific configuration, conventionally clean: flaps up, gear up, power off. Vso is always the lower of the two, because flaps let the wing fly slower.

Where you find it without opening a book

The bottom of the white arc on the airspeed indicator. Vs1 is the bottom of the green arc.

The qualifier that sets up question 18

Vso is published at maximum gross weight. Stall speed is not a fixed property of the airplane — it goes down as weight goes down, and up with load factor in a turn. Solo with half tanks, your airplane actually stalls meaningfully below the published Vso. Hold on to that, because the next question but one depends on it.

Two derived numbers you should have

Vso is the reference for both. Your final approach speed is typically about 1.3 × Vso, and the FAA's short-field runway-length guidance uses 1.3 Vso over the threshold. Know what 1.3 Vso is for your airplane.

17

What is the definition and speed of Vr?

5 pts · Short answer

Rotation speed — the speed on the takeoff roll at which you apply back pressure to raise the nosewheel and rotate to the takeoff attitude.

Your number: —

The honest caveat

Vr is a transport-category certification speed. Light singles often don't publish it as a marked limitation at all — instead the POH's takeoff procedure says something like "lift off at 55 KIAS." That number is your Vr. Look in POH Section 4, Normal Procedures, and note that the short-field takeoff usually specifies a different, lower rotation speed than the normal takeoff.

Why rotating at the wrong speed hurts

  • Too early — high angle of attack at low airspeed, enormous induced drag, and the airplane staggers off in ground effect without enough energy to climb out of it. This is a classic accident.
  • Too late — you use runway you didn't need to use, and you wheelbarrow on the nosewheel, which is neither strong nor steerable in that regime.
18

Can Vr be less than Vso?

5 pts · Yes / No

Yes  ·  No

Yes. This is the trick question on the form, and it works because people treat Vso as a hard floor. It isn't — it's a number measured under one specific set of conditions.

Three reasons, and you should be able to give all three

  1. Vso is published at maximum gross weight. Stall speed varies with the square root of weight. Fly solo with partial fuel and the wing genuinely stalls several knots below the book Vso — so a rotation speed below the published number can still be above the actual stall speed that day.
  2. Ground effect. Within about one wingspan of the surface, the ground interferes with the wingtip vortices, cutting induced drag and increasing effective lift for the same angle of attack. An airplane can and does become airborne in ground effect below its free-air stall speed — and then settles back on when it climbs out of the cushion. That's the whole mechanism behind the soft-field takeoff.
  3. Configuration and units. Vso is measured in the landing configuration, but you take off in a different one. And Vso is often published in KCAS while your rotation speed is read in KIAS — near the stall, the position error between the two is at its largest, so you can be comparing two numbers that aren't on the same scale.

Where this lives in real flying

The soft-field takeoff. Full aft elevator, the airplane lifts off at the lowest possible speed — below Vso — and then you lower the nose and accelerate in ground effect to Vx or Vy before climbing. If you pull it out of ground effect early, the wing quits. This is the question, dressed up as a definition.

Before you write it down

Answer keys on homemade forms vary. Write Yes and give the ground-effect and weight reasoning — that reasoning is correct regardless of what the key says, and it's what your CFI actually wants to hear at the oral. If they mark it wrong, you have the conversation, and that conversation is worth more than the five points.

19

What is the "V" in V-speed?

5 pts · Short answer

Vitesse — French for speed (or velocity).

The convention comes from early international aviation standards work, which was conducted largely in French. It's why every one of these is a V followed by a subscript, and why the letter never stands for anything English no matter how hard people try to make "velocity" fit.

Five free points

This is a gift question with exactly one right answer. Don't overthink it and don't write "velocity" — write vitesse, and note it's French.

Regs & scenarios

Three yes/no or multiple-choice questions where the reasoning is the whole point.

20

You own a coffee bean company…

5 pts · Yes / No

Yes  ·  No  — read the note

Heads up

The full wording of this scenario didn't come through when I read the form. It's the classic setup — can you use the airplane to move your product, or fly for your business? — so here's the rule that governs it. Read the actual question on the form carefully before answering.

For a student pilot, the answer to essentially every version of this is No. §61.89 is unusually blunt.

§61.89 — Student pilot limitations

A student pilot may not act as pilot in command of an aircraft:

  • That is carrying a passenger;
  • That is carrying property for compensation or hire;
  • For compensation or hire;
  • In furtherance of a business;
  • On an international flight (with narrow exceptions);
  • With a flight or surface visibility below 3 SM by day / 5 SM by night;
  • When unable to see the surface by visual reference;
  • In a manner contrary to any limitation in their logbook endorsements.

"In furtherance of a business" is the wide one

It doesn't require that you be paid. Flying your own beans to your own shop, flying to a supplier meeting, flying an employee anywhere — all of it advances the business, and all of it is out. Even flying empty to a meeting you're attending for the company is in furtherance of the business.

How this changes when you get the private certificate

Under §61.113(a), a private pilot still may not act as PIC for compensation or hire. But §61.113(b) carves out the case you're being set up for: a private pilot may fly in connection with a business or employment if the flight is only incidental to that business, and the aircraft carries no passengers or property for compensation or hire. The test is whether the flight itself is the thing of value being sold. If you'd have made the trip by car and the flying is just how you got there, it's incidental. If someone is buying the transportation, it isn't.

21

You preflight the airplane and find the navigation light inoperative…

5 pts · Yes / No

Yes  ·  No  — depends on the wording

Heads up

The rest of this question didn't come through in full — it hinges on whether the flight is day or night, so check that on the form. As a student on a solo, the practical answer is almost always No, don't go — but know the flow, because that's what earns the points.

Position (navigation) lights are required for night flight, not for day VFR. So the reg answer differs — but the student answer doesn't.

§91.205 — what has to work

Day VFR (§91.205(b)) — A TOMATO FLAMES: Altimeter, Tachometer, Oil pressure gauge, Manifold pressure (if applicable), Airspeed indicator, Temperature gauge (liquid-cooled), Oil temperature (air-cooled), Fuel gauge, Landing gear position lights, Anticollision lights, Magnetic compass, ELT, Seatbelts. Position lights are not on this list.

Night VFR (§91.205(c)) adds FLAPS: Fuses (spares), Landing light (only if for hire), Anticollision lights, Position lights, Source of power. Now it's required.

§91.213(d) — the inoperative-equipment flow for a light single with no MEL

  1. Is it required by the VFR-day type certification (the KOEL / equipment list in POH Section 6)?
  2. Is it required by §91.205 for the kind of flight you're making?
  3. Is it required by an airworthiness directive?
  4. If no to all three: it must be deactivated or removed and placarded "INOPERATIVE" — and the removal/deactivation is maintenance, which means a certificated mechanic or repairman does it and makes the logbook entry. Then the PIC determines whether the aircraft is still safe to fly.
  5. If yes to any: the airplane is not airworthy for that flight. It doesn't go.

The answer to give as a student

"No — I don't go. Even if it's a day VFR flight where position lights aren't required by §91.205, the discrepancy has to be deactivated and placarded by maintenance before it's legal, and that's not something I can do or sign off. I'd write it up in the squawk sheet and call my instructor and dispatch."

Nobody has ever been criticized for grounding an airplane over a light. As a solo student, "when in doubt, it doesn't fly" is both the right answer and the right instinct.

You are in the traffic pattern and your radios go out…

5 pts · Multiple choice

Cleared to land  ·  Cleared for the option  ·  Depart the airspace immediately  ·  Use extreme caution

The four options are light gun signal meanings — so the question is asking what a particular light from the tower tells you. The one you'll be given in the pattern is steady green: cleared to land.

What you actually do first

  1. Squawk 7600 — the transponder code for lost communications.
  2. Stay in the pattern and keep flying the airplane. Don't leave, don't do anything abrupt. The tower can see you.
  3. Watch the tower for the light gun.
  4. Acknowledge: rock your wings by day, blink your landing light at night.
  5. Troubleshoot only after that: volume, squelch, frequency, the audio panel, headset jacks, the comm circuit breaker, the second radio, the handheld if there's one aboard.

The full table — memorize this

SignalIn flightOn the ground
Steady greenCleared to landCleared for takeoff
Flashing greenReturn for landing — a steady green will followCleared to taxi
Steady redGive way to other aircraft and continue circlingStop
Flashing redAirport unsafe — do not landTaxi clear of the runway in use
Flashing white—Return to your starting point on the airport
Alternating red/greenGeneral warning — exercise extreme caution

A memory hook that survives the checkride

Green means go, red means no — in both columns. Steady = now, flashing = not yet / go somewhere else. Flashing white only ever appears on the ground, because "return to your starting point" is meaningless in the air. Alternating red and green is the only signal that means the same thing in both columns, and it never means "cleared" for anything.

You're allowed to be there

§91.126–§91.131 require two-way radio communication for Class D — but §91.129(d)(2) provides for exactly this: if the radio fails in flight, you may continue to the airport, enter the pattern, and land, provided you have the tower's light gun clearance and weather permits. A radio failure inbound is different from one that existed before you departed.

23

Complete a weight and balance

3 pts · Short answer + file upload

The form asks for eight specific outputs. Here's what each one is and where it comes from.

Required outputWhat it is / where it comes from
Crew weightYou, plus your CFI if they're aboard. Actual weight with what you're wearing and carrying — not your driver's license weight.
Maximum fuel weightUsable fuel × 6 lb/gal for 100LL. Then check: does full fuel plus crew exceed max gross? On many trainers with two adults it does — that's the point of the question.
Zero fuel weightEverything except fuel: empty weight + crew + baggage. The floor your weight can never drop below in flight.
Projected landing weightTakeoff weight − fuel burned. Estimate the burn from POH Section 5 for your planned time aloft, and re-check the CG at that weight — burning fuel moves it.
Takeoff distance — ground rollPOH Section 5. Interpolate for pressure altitude and temperature.
Takeoff over a 50 ft obstacleSame table, second column. Always the larger number.
Landing distance — ground rollPOH Section 5 landing chart.
Landing over a 50 ft obstacleSame chart, second column.

The arithmetic, in one line

Moment = Weight × Arm   →   CG = Total Moment ÷ Total Weight

Build the table — empty aircraft, front seats, rear seats, baggage area 1, baggage area 2, fuel — with a weight, an arm, and a moment on each row. Sum the weight column and the moment column, divide, and plot the result on the CG envelope in POH Section 6. Two answers matter: is total weight at or below max gross, and does the CG fall inside the envelope? Both must be yes. Use the weight and balance sheet for your specific tail number — empty weight and arm differ airplane to airplane and change when equipment changes.

Corrections that make the distances real

  • Pressure altitude — set 29.92 in the altimeter and read it, or compute it. Not field elevation.
  • Temperature — hot air is thin air. Interpolate; don't round in your favor.
  • Wind — the chart notes tell you how much to subtract per knot of headwind and, more importantly, add per knot of tailwind. The tailwind penalty is brutal.
  • Surface — dry grass typically adds ~15% to the ground roll; the exact figure is in the chart notes.
  • Then add your own margin. The book numbers were flown by a factory test pilot in a new airplane. Give yourself at least 50%.

Know what being out of limits does

Overweight — longer takeoff roll, worse climb, higher stall speed, longer landing roll, reduced structural margin.
CG too far forward — nose-heavy, higher stall speed, needs more elevator to flare, may be impossible to round out; worse cruise performance.
CG too far aft — the dangerous one. Unstable in pitch, and it can make the airplane unrecoverable from a spin. If you're near the aft limit, know it.

Practical

Do this on paper or in ForeFlight, photograph or export it, and have the file ready before you open the form — the question wants an upload and you don't want to be scrambling.

Bring with you

0 pts · Checkboxes

Student pilot certificate  ·  Medical  ·  Logbook

Zero points, and the single highest-consequence question on the form. Without the logbook, your instructor cannot write the endorsement, and you do not fly.

☐Student pilot certificate — the plastic card. Not the application, not a screenshot of IACRA.
☐Medical certificate — check the expiration date tonight, not in the morning. Third class medicals expire on the last day of the month.
☐Logbook — this is where the §61.87 pre-solo endorsement and the 90-day solo endorsement get written. No logbook, no endorsement, no solo. Put it in the bag now.

Not on the list, but bring it anyway

  • Government photo ID — required by §61.3(a)(2) and easy to leave behind.
  • Current sectional (paper or the EFB, and a charged device with a backup power source).
  • Headset, kneeboard, two pens, and your completed weight and balance.
  • Water. You'll be more nervous and more dehydrated than you expect, and both degrade your flying.

One more thing

Your instructor is not going to sign you off unless they're confident. If the weather is wrong or the day feels wrong, saying so is a mark of judgment, not a failure — and judgment is the thing a solo endorsement is actually certifying. The airplane will still be there next week.
